§1Snow relational structures

A relational structure $(S, \set{R_\alpha}_{\alpha \in A})$ is a set $S$ along with subsets $R_\alpha \subseteq S^{n_\alpha}$, where $n_\alpha \in \N$. A homomorphism of relational structures $f : (S, \set{R_\alpha}_{\alpha \in A}) \to (S', \set{R'_\alpha}_{\alpha \in A})$ is a function $f : S \to S'$ such that for all $\alpha \in A$, if $(x_1, \dots, x_{n_\alpha}) \in R_\alpha$ then $(f(x_1), \dots, f(x_{n_\alpha})) \in R'_\alpha$.

For a relation $R \subseteq S^n$ and subset $S' \subseteq S$, we denote $R \cap S'^n$ by $R\rvert_{S'}$, called the restriction of $R$ to $S'$.

1.1Snow monoids

A monoid $(M, \set{E, T})$ is a relational structure where

  1. $E \subseteq M$;
  2. $T \subseteq M \times M \times M$

satisfying:

  1. there exists a unique $1_M \in M$ such that $1_M \in E$;
  2. for all $x \in M$, $(1_M, x, x) \in T$ and $(x, 1_M, x) \in T$;
  3. for all $x, y \in M$, there exists a unique $z \in M$ such that $(x, y, z) \in T$;
  4. for all $x, y, z, u, v, w \in M$, if $(x, y, u) \in T$, $(u, z, w) \in T$, $(y, z, v) \in T$ then $(x, v, w) \in T$.

1.2Snow groups

A group $(G, \set{E, T})$ is a monoid further satisfying:

  • for all $x \in G$, there exists $y \in G$ such that $(x, y, 1_G) \in T$ and $(y, x, 1_G) \in T$.

An abelian group $(G, \set{E, T})$ is a group further satisfying:

  • for all $x, y, z \in G$, if $(x, y, z) \in T$ then $(y, x, z) \in T$.

1.3Snow rings

A ring $(R, \set{Z, O, A, M})$ is a relational structure where

  1. $(R, \set{Z, A})$ is an abelian group;
  2. $(R, \set{O, M})$ is a monoid;

satisfying:

  1. for all $x, y, z, p, q, r, s \in R$, if $(y, z, s) \in A$, $(x, y, p) \in M$, $(x, z, q) \in M$, $(p, q, r) \in A$ then $(x, s, r) \in M$;
  2. for all $x, y, z, p, q, r, s \in R$, if $(x, y, s) \in A$, $(x, z, p) \in M$, $(y, z, q) \in M$, $(p, q, r) \in A$ then $(s, z, r) \in M$.

A field is a ring where $(R \setminus \set0, \set{O\rvert_{R \setminus \set0}, M\rvert_{R \setminus \set0}})$ is a group.

§2Algebraic structures

2.1Monoids

A monoid $(M,*)$ is a set $M$ equipped with a binary operation $*: M\times M\to M$ satisfying the following axioms:

  1. Identity: There exists $1_M\in M$ such that $1_M*m=m*1_M=m$ for all $m \in M$, called the identity of $M$.
  2. Associativity: For all $x,y,z\in M$, $x*(y*z)=(x*y)*z$.

Often, we refer to the monoid with the same name as its underlying set, which is $M$ in this case. It is also common to represent the monoid operation by concatenation. Due to associativity, parentheses are not needed. For example $xyz=x*(y*z)=(x*y)*z$.

A monoid is commutative if it also obeys the following:

  1. Commutativity: For all $x,y\in M$, $x*y=y*x$.

A group is a monoid with inverses:

  1. Inverses: For all $x \in M$, there exists $x^{-1}\in M$ such that $x*x^{-1}=x^{-1}*x=1_M$.

An abelian group is a commutative group.

Typically the binary operation in a commutative monoid $M$ is denoted by the addition symbol $+$, and the identity element is denoted by $0_M$ instead of $1_M$. In an abelian group, inverses are then denoted by $-x$ instead of $x^{-1}$.

2.2Rings

An algebra $(A,+,*)$ is a set $A$ equipped with two binary operations such that:

  1. $(A,+)$ is an abelian group; $+$ is called the additive operation, or simply addition.
  2. $(A,*)$ is a monoid; $*$ is called the multiplicative operation, or simply multiplication.
  3. Distributivity: For all $a,b,c\in A$, $a*(b+c)=a*b+a*c$ and $(a+b)*c = a*c + b*c$.

Typically, we call the additive identity $0_A$ and the multiplicative identity $1_A$.

A ring $(R,+,*)$ is an algebra such that $(R,*)$ is a commutative monoid.

A field is a ring $(R,+,*)$ for which $(R\setminus\set0,*)$ is a group.

Let $(R, +_R, *_R)$ be a ring. The polynomial ring in one indeterminate $x$ with coefficients in $R$ is the ring denoted $R[x]$ whose elements are polynomials \[ p(x) = a_0 + a_1x + a_2x^2 + a_3x^3 + \dots + a_nx^n, \quad n \in \N, a_i \in R, \] and the operations are defined as follows:

  1. The symbol $x^0$ is defined to be $1_R$.
  2. $\sum_{i = 0}^n a_i x^i + \sum_{i = 0}^n b_i x^i = \sum_{i = 0}^n (a_i +_R b_i) x^i$.
  3. $\bigl(\sum_{i = 0}^n a_i x^i\bigr) * \bigl(\sum_{i = 0}^n b_i x^i\bigr) = \sum_{i = 0}^{2n} c_ix^i$, where $c_i = \sum_{j = 0}^i a_j *_R b_{i - j}$.
  1. Let $R = (R, +, *)$ be a ring. Prove that $R[x]$ has infinitely many elements.
  2. Prove that there are finitely many functions $f : R \to R$ if $R$ is a finite set.

2.3Homomorphisms

If $(M, *_M)$ and $(N, *_N)$ are monoids, then a monoid homomorphism from $M$ to $N$ is a function $\varphi:M\to N$ such that

  1. $\varphi(1_M)=1_N$.
  2. $\varphi(a*_Mb)=\varphi(a)*_N\varphi(b)$ for all $a, b \in M$.

A monoid homomorphism between groups is a group homomorphism. A function between algebras that is a homomorphism of both the additive groups and multiplicative monoids is called an algebra homomorphism, and an algebra homomorphism of rings is called a ring homomorphism. Naturally, a ring homomorphism between fields is a field homomorphism.

A monoid, group, algebra, ring, or field homomorphism is an isomorphism if it is a bijection.

Let $\Phi : M \to N$ be a monoid isomorphism. Prove that the inverse $\Phi^{-1} : N \to M$ is also a monoid isomorphism.

The identity function $\id_M : M \to M$ of a monoid $M$ given by $\id_M(a) = a$ is always a homomorphism, called the identity homomorphism.

Let $\varphi : M \to N$ be a monoid homomorphism.

  1. The kernel of $\varphi$ is $\ker\varphi = \set {\, m \in M \mid \varphi(m) = 1_N \,}$.
  2. The image of $\varphi$ is $\im\varphi = \set {\, n \in N \mid \exists m \in M, \varphi(m) = n \,}$.

Kernels and images of group homomorphisms are defined analogously. Kernels and images of algebra homomorphisms are taken using the additive structure.

Let $R$ be a ring. The evaluation homomorphism at $t \in R$ is the homomorphism \[ \ev_t : R[x] \to R, \quad \ev_t(a_0 + a_1x + \dots + a_nx^n) = a_0 + a_1t + \dots + a_nt^n. \] For a polynomial $p(x) \in R[x]$, we write $p(t) \coloneq \ev_t(p(x))$.

Let $X$ be a set. We write $R^X$ for the set of functions $X \to R$. It is a ring when given the operations

  1. $(f + g)(x) = f(x) + g(x)$,
  2. $(f * g)(x) = f(x) * g(x)$.

Prove that the function $\Phi : R[x] \to R^R, \Phi(p(x))(t) = p(t)$ is a homomorphism of rings.

2.4Additional concepts

Monoids, groups, rings, and fields are examples of algebraic structures.

A homomorphism $\varphi : S \to S$ from an algebraic structure to itself is called an endomorphism of that structure.

The endomorphism algebra of an abelian group $M=(M,+)$ is the set of all endomorphisms of $M$, and is denoted $\End(M)$. The addition operation is $(\varphi+\psi)(x)=\varphi(x)+\psi(x)$, and the multiplication operation is $(\varphi*\psi)(x)=\varphi(\psi(x))$.

A submonoid of a monoid $M$ is a subset $N \subseteq M$ which satisfies:

  1. Identity: $1_M \in N$.
  2. Closure: For all $x, y \in N$, $x * y \in N$.

A submonoid of a group which is also a group is called a subgroup. A subring is a subset of a ring which is both a subgroup under the addition operation and submonoid under the multiplication operation, and a subfield is a field which is a subring of another field.

The trivial monoid is the monoid with only the identity element $\set{1}$. This is also a group, called the trivial group. The trivial ring is the ring with only one element $\set{0}$.

When using multiplicative notation, the trivial monoid and trivial group are often denoted by $1$, without the curly braces. When using additive notation, the trivial monoid and trivial group are often denoted by $0$, without the curly braces. The trivial ring is often denoted by $0$.

2.5Exercises

  1. Show that the identity of a monoid is unique.
    Solution ★★

    Suppose $a,b$ are identities of $M$. By the identity property of $a$, $ab=b$, but by the identity property of $b$, $ab=a$. Thus, $a=b$.

  2. If $g,h$ are elements of a monoid $M$ such that $gh=1_M$, then $g$ is said to be the left-inverse of $h$, and $h$ is the right-inverse of $g$.
    1. Show that if an element of $M$ has both a left-inverse and a right-inverse, the left inverse and right inverse must be equal.
      Solution ★★

      Let $g$ have left-inverse $a$ and right-inverse $b$. Then, consider $agb$. Since $ag$ is the identity, $agb=b$, but since $gb$ is the identity, $agb=a$. Thus, $a=b$.

    2. Conclude that in a group, inverses are unique.
      Solution ★★

      Since group inverses are double-sided, if an element has two inverses, we can choose one to be a left-inverse and one to be a right-inverse, then apply part a.

    3. Give an example of a monoid where one element has more than one right inverse.
      Solution ★★★

      Let $M=\{f:\N\to\N\}$ with operation $f*g=g\circ f$. If $f(x)=x+1$ then any function $g$ for which $g(x)=x-1$ for $x\geq1$ is a right-inverse to $f$. There are infinitely many of these, one for every choice of $g(0)$.

  3. Show that a monoid where every element is right invertible is a group.
    Solution ★★★

    Let $a$ be an arbitrary element in the monoid. Let $b$ be a right inverse of $a$ and $c$ be a right inverse of $b$. Then, $abc = (ab)c = c = a(bc) = a$. Since $a=c$ and $c$ has left-inverse $b$, $a$ has left-inverse $b$.

  4. Suppose we replace the identity axiom with the existence of a right identity $1_M$ such that for all $x$, $x*1_M=x$. Show that even then, if every element of $M$ is right-invertible then it is a group. (Note: a right identity and left inverses (or vice versa) is not a strong enough condition to be a group).
    Solution ★★★★

    As with the previous exercise, let $a$ be arbitrary, $b$ a right-inverse of $a$, and $c$ a right inverse of $b$. Let $1_M$ be a right-identity for $M$. Then, $abc=a(bc)=a1_M=a=(ab)c=1_Mc$. Then, $ba=b(1_Mc)=(b1_M)c=bc=1$, hence $a$ has left inverse $b$.

  5. Call a monoid right-cancellative if for all $a,b,x$, $ax=bx$ implies $a=b$. Show every finite right-cancellative monoid is a group.
    Solution ★★★

    Right-cancellativity implies that $f(x)=xm$ is injective for all $m$. Since $f$ is a function on the monoid, which is finite, this implies surjectivity, which means there exists an $x$ such that $xm=1_M$. Then, every element is left-invertible, reducing to a mirrored version of Exercise 3.

  6. Let $M$ be a finite monoid, and let $a,b\in M$ such that $ab=1_M$. Show $ba=1_M$.
    Solution ★★★

    Like the problem above, we consider $f(x) = xa$, and show this is injective, which then implies surjectivity due to the finiteness of the monoid. Suppose $f(x) = f(y)$, then we have $$\begin{aligned} xa &= ya \\ xab &= yab \\ x * 1_M &= y * 1_M \\ x &= y \end{aligned}$$ Since $f$ is surjective, we have that $1_M = h * a$ for some $h \in M$. Right multiply both sides by $b$ and get $b = h$.

  7. Let $G$ be a group, and let $S$ and $T$ be subsets of $G$ such that $|S|+|T|>|G|$. Define $ST\coloneq\set{\,st\mid s\in S,t\in T\,}$. Show $G=ST$.
  8. Show that in a ring, $0*x=0$.
  9. Show that the composition of two homomorphisms is a homomorphism.
  10. Show that a group $G$ is abelian if and only if $\phi(g)=g^{-1}$ is a homomorphism.
    Solution ★

    $\To$) $\phi(ab) = (ab)^{-1} = \underbrace{b^{-1} a^{-1} = a^{-1} b^{-1}}_{\text{abelian}} = \phi(a)\phi(b)$

    $\From$) $$\begin{aligned} \phi(a^{-1}b^{-1}) &= \phi(a^{-1})\phi(b^{-1}) \\ (a^{-1}b^{-1})^{-1} &= ab \\ ba &= ab \end{aligned}$$

  11. Show that a group $G$ is abelian if and only if $\phi(g)=g^2$ is a homomorphism. Why does this imply non-abelian groups cannot have endomorphism rings?
    Solution ★★

    $\To$) $\phi(ab) = (ab)^2 = abab = a^2 b^2 = \phi(a)\phi(b)$.

    $\From$) $$\begin{aligned} \phi(ab) &= \phi(a)\phi(b) \\ (ab)^2 &= a^2 b^2 \\ abab &= a^2 b^2 \\ \textcolor{#ff5a5f}{a^{-1}} abab \textcolor{#ff5a5f}{b^{-1}} &= \textcolor{#ff5a5f}{a^{-1}} a^2 b^2 \textcolor{#ff5a5f}{b^{-1}} \\ ba &= ab \end{aligned}$$

    In $\End(G)$, we already have multiplication: it's just function composition. To be a ring, we need addition and it needs to preserve homomorphisms. So, given $f,h \in \End(G)$, $(f+h)(g) = f(g) *_G h(g)$. Consider the identity $1_{\End(G)}$ as both $f$ and $h$. Then, we have $(1_{\End(G)} + 1_{\End(G)})(g) = g^2$. This has to be a homomorphism for addition to make sense, which means $G$ must be abelian.

  12. Let $M$ be a monoid.
    1. Let $N, P \subseteq M$ be submonoids. Show that $N \cap P$ is a submonoid of $M$.
    2. Let $N_1, N_2, \dots, N_n \subseteq M$ be submonoids. Show that $N_1 \cap N_2 \cap \dots \cap N_n$ is a submonoid of $M$.
    3. Let $\NN$ be a collection of submonoids of $M$. Show that $\bigcap_{N \in \NN} N$ is a submonoid of $M$.
    4. Let $N_1 \subseteq N_2 \subseteq N_3 \subseteq \dots \subseteq M$ be an increasing sequence of submonoids. Show that $\bigcup_{n \in \N} N_n$ is a submonoid of $M$.
    5. Prove the above four results for subgroups, subalgebras, subrings, and subfields.
  13. Let $M$ be a commutative monoid.
    1. Let $N, P \subseteq M$ be submonoids. Show that $N + P \coloneq \set {\, n + p \mid n \in N, p \in P\,}$ is a submonoid of $M$.
    2. Let $N_1, N_2, \dots, N_n \subseteq M$ be submonoids. Show that $N_1 + N_2 + \dots + N_n \coloneq \set {\, n_1 + n_2 + \dots + n_n \mid n_i \in N_i \,}$ is a submonoid of $M$.
    3. Prove the above two results for subgroups of abelian groups.

§3New abelian groups from old

3.1Direct sums

Let $(A, +_A)$ and $(B, +_B)$ be abelian groups. Their direct sum, denoted by $A \oplus B$, is the abelian group whose underlying set is $A \times B$ and addition operation is $(a_1, b_1) + (a_2, b_2) = (a_1 +_A a_2, b_1 +_B b_2)$.

Given a sequence of abelian groups $(A_1, +_{A_1}), \dots, (A_n, +_{A_n})$, their direct sum, denoted $A_1 \oplus A_2 \oplus \dots \oplus A_n$, is the abelian group whose underlying set is $A_1 \times A_2 \times \dots \times A_n$ and addition operation is \[ (a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n) + (a_1', a_2', \dots, a_n') = (a_1 +_{A_1} a_1', a_2 +_{A_2} a_2', \dots, a_n +_{A_n} a_n'). \]

The $n$-fold direct sum of an abelian group $(A, +_A)$ with itself will be denoted $A^{\oplus n}$, i.e., $A^{\oplus n} = A \oplus A \oplus \dots \oplus A$, where $A$ appears $n$ times. Its underlying set is the $n$-fold cartesian product $A^n$.

Given a direct sum $A \oplus B$, there are always four homomorphisms:

  • The inclusions $i_A : A \to A \oplus B, i_A(a) = (a, 0)$ and $i_B : B \to A \oplus B, i_B(b) = (0, b)$.
  • The projections $p_A : A \oplus B \to A, p_A(a, b) = a$ and $p_B : A \oplus B \to B, p_B(a, b) = b$.

Notice that the compositions $p_A \circ i_A : A \to A$ and $p_B \circ i_B : B \to B$ are identity maps, therefore making both $i_A$ and $i_B$ injective, and $p_A$ and $p_B$ surjective.

Let $A$ and $B$ be abelian groups. Show the following:

  1. For any abelian group $C$ and homomorphisms $\varphi_A : C \to A$ and $\varphi_B : C \to B$, there exists a unique homomorphism $\Phi : C \to A \oplus B$ such that $p_A \circ \Phi = \varphi_A$ and $p_B \circ \Phi = \varphi_B$. The unique homomorphism $\Phi : C \to A \oplus B$ is denoted $\varphi_A \times \varphi_B$, called the product of $\varphi_A$ and $\varphi_B$.
  2. For any abelian group $C$ and homomorphisms $\psi_A : A \to C$ and $\psi_B : B \to C$, there exists a unique homomorphism $\Psi : A \oplus B \to C$ such that $\Psi \circ i_A = \psi_A$ and $\Psi \circ i_B = \psi_B$. The unique homomorphism $\Psi : A \oplus B \to C$ is denoted $\psi_A \amalg \psi_B$, called the coproduct of $\psi_A$ and $\psi_B$.

3.2Quotients

Let $(A, +_A)$ be an abelian group, and let $B \subseteq A$ be a subgroup. A translate of $B$ by an element $a \in A$ is the subset \[ a +_A B = \set{\,a +_A b \mid b \in B\,}. \] Notice that for any $b \in B$, $(a +_A b) +_A B = a +_A B$. The set of all translates of $B$ is denoted $A/B$.

The set of all translates $A/B$ can be made into an abelian group, called the quotient of $A$ by $B$, or $A$ mod $B$, by defining the addition operation between two translates $a_1 +_A B$ and $a_2 +_A B$ to be \[ (a_1 +_A B) +_{A/B} (a_2 +_A B) = (a_1 +_A a_2) +_A B. \]

Check that the above definition actually does make $A/B$ into an abelian group.

Given a quotient $A/B$, there is always a quotient homomorphism $q_{A/B} : A \to A/B, q(a) = a +_A B$. Notice that this is always surjective.

Let $A$ be an abelian group and $B \subseteq A$ a subgroup. Prove that for any abelian group $C$ and homomorphism $\varphi : A \to C$ such that $B \subseteq \ker\varphi$, there exists a unique homomorphism $\ol\varphi : A/B \to C$ such that $\ol\varphi \circ q_{A/B} = \varphi$. The homomorphism $\ol\varphi$ is often called the induced homomorphism on the quotient $A/B$.

Let $\varphi : A \to B$ be a homomorphism of abelian groups. The cokernel of $\varphi$ is the quotient $\coker\varphi = B/{\im\varphi}$.

§4Vector spaces

Let $F$ be a field. An $F$-vector space $(V, \rho)$ is an abelian group $V$ equipped with an algebra homomorphism $\rho: F \to \End(V)$. Often, we refer to the whole vector space simply as $V$, and we say that an $F$-vector space is over $F$.

For brevity, $\rho(a)(v)$ for $a \in F$ and $v \in V$ is typically denoted $av$, with $\rho$ left implicit. This operation is called scalar multiplication, because one typically calls $F$ the field of scalars.

Note that because $V$ is an abelian group, we will use $0_V$ to denote its identity element, and use additive notation. So a typical equation involving elements of $V$ and $F$ might look like $ax + by = 0$, where $a, b \in F$ and $x, y \in V$.

Let $V$ and $W$ be $F$-vector spaces. A linear transformation (or simply, linear map) from $(V, \rho_V)$ to $(W, \rho_W)$ is an abelian group homomorphism $f:V\to W$ such that for all $a\in F$, \[ f\circ\rho_V(a)=\rho_W(a)\circ f. \]

A category theorist would say that this means that the following diagram commutes for all $a \in F$.

This is a visual way to show that applying $f$ and then $\rho_W(a)$ is the same as applying $\rho_V(a)$ and then $f$.

A linear transformation between vector spaces is an isomorphism if it is an isomorphism of abelian groups.

Prove that the inverse of an isomorphism of vector spaces is also a linear transformation.

A subspace of a vector space $(V, \rho_V)$ over $F$ is a subgroup of $V$ which is closed under application of $\rho_V(a)$ for all $a \in F$.

§5Dimension and bases

5.1Bases using flags

Let $V$ be a vector space. A flag of $V$ is a sequence of subspaces $V_0, V_1, \dots, V_n \subseteq V$ such that \[ 0 = V_0 \subsetneq V_1 \subsetneq \dots \subsetneq V_n = V. \] The integer $n$ is called the length of the flag. Notice that the indices start from $0$.

The dimension of a vector space $V$ is the maximum length of a flag of $V$, if it exists; or is said to be infinite otherwise. This is denoted by $\dim V$, or $\dim_F V$ to emphasize the field.

When the dimension of $V$ is finite, we say that $V$ is finite-dimensional. Otherwise, we say it is infinite-dimensional, and we write $\dim V = \infty$.

A sequence of elements $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n \in V$ is linearly independent if there exists a flag $V_0, V_1, \dots, V_n \subseteq V$ with $v_i \in V_i \setminus V_{i - 1}$ for all $i = 1, \dots, n$. Otherwise, we say that $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ are linearly dependent.

A basis of $V$ is a linearly independent sequence $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n \in V$ such that $n = \dim V$.

Let $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n \in V$ be a sequence of elements. Their span, denoted $\spn\set{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n}$, is the smallest subspace of $V$ that contains the subset $\set {v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n}$. The span of $v \in V$ may be denoted $Fv$.

Given a subspace $W \subseteq V$, the elements $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ are said to span $W$, or be spanning $W$, if their span equals $W$. If $W = V$, one may simply say that they span, or are spanning.

Here, a subspace $U \subseteq V$ is said to be smaller than another subspace $W \subseteq V$ if $U \subsetneq W$.

5.2Exercises

5.3Bases and coordinates

Consider the vector space $(F^{\oplus n}, \rho)$ over $F$ with the field action $\rho(a)(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) = (ax_1, ax_2, \dots, ax_n)$. The standard basis of $F^{\oplus n}$ is the sequence $e_1, e_2, \dots, e_n$, where $e_i = (0, \dots, 0, 1, 0, \dots, 0)$ is the element whose coordinates are all $0$ except for a single $1$ in the $i$th slot.

The $i$th coordinate map of $F^{\oplus n}$ is the linear transformation $e_i^\dual : F^{\oplus n} \to F$ defined by $e_i^\dual(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) = x_i$. Therefore, \[ e_i^\dual(e_j) = \begin{dcases} 1 & \text{if } i = j, \\ 0 & \text{if } i \ne j. \end{dcases} \]

Let $V$ be a vector space and $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n \in V$. We define $\Phi_{(v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n)} : F^{\oplus n} \to V$ to be the linear map given by $(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) \mapsto x_1v_1 + x_2v_2 + \dots + x_n v_n$.

Let $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n \in V$, and let $\Phi_{(v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n)} : F^{\oplus n} \to V$ be the linear map defined above.

  1. Prove that $\Phi_{(v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n)}$ is injective if and only if $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ is linearly independent.
  2. Prove that $\Phi_{(v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n)}$ is surjective if and only if $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ spans $V$.
  3. Prove that $\Phi_{(v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n)}$ is an isomorphism if and only if $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ is a basis of $V$.

Let $B = (v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n)$ be a basis of $V$, and let $\Phi_{(v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n)} : F^{\oplus n} \to V$ be the resulting isomorphism. The coordinate vector of an element $v \in V$ with respect to the basis $B$ is the element of $F^{\oplus n}$ denoted $[v]_B \coloneq \Phi_{(v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n)}^{-1}(v)$.

The $i$th coordinate map with respect to $B$ is the composition $v_i^\dual \coloneq e_i^\dual \circ \Phi_{(v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n)}^{-1} : V \to F$. The $i$th coordinate of an element $v \in V$ with respect to $B$ is $v_i^\dual(v)$. Therefore, \[ v_i^\dual(v_j) = \begin{dcases} 1 & \text{if } i = j, \\ 0 & \text{if } i \ne j. \end{dcases} \]

§6New vector spaces from old

Let $V=(V, \rho_V)$ and $W=(W, \rho_W)$ be vector spaces. The (external) direct sum of $V$ and $W$, denoted $V \oplus W$, is the abelian group $V \oplus W$ equipped with a field action $\rho(a)(v,w) = (\rho_V(a)(v), \rho_W(a)(w))$.

Let $V$ be a vector space and $W_1, W_2, \dots, W_n \subseteq V$ be subspaces. There is a linear transformation \[ \Phi : W_1 \oplus W_2 \oplus \dots \oplus W_n \to W_1 + W_2 + \dots + W_n, \quad \Phi(w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n) = w_1 + w_2 + \dots + w_n. \] The subspaces $W_1, W_2, \dots, W_n$ are said to be in internal direct sum if $\Phi$ is an isomorphism, and we write $W_1 \oplus W_2 \oplus \dots \oplus W_n$ for the subspace $W_1 + W_2 + \dots + W_n$ of $V$.

Technically, we have now defined $W_1 \oplus W_2 \oplus \dots \oplus W_n$ to mean two different things. But because there is an obvious isomorphism between the external and internal direct sums, no confusion shall arise.

Let $V = (V, \rho_V)$ be a vector space and $W \subseteq V$ a subspace. The quotient of $V$ by $W$, denoted $V/W$, is the abelian group $V/W$ equipped with a field action $\rho(a)(v + W) = \rho_V(a)(v) + W$.

Let $V=(V, \rho_V)$ and $W=(W, \rho_W)$ be vector spaces. The homset between $V$ and $W$ is the set of all linear transformations $V \to W$, and is denoted $\Hom(V, W)$. This is an abelian group with addition operation $(f + g)(v) = f(v) + g(v)$, and the field action is $(\rho(a)f)(v) = f(\rho_V(a)(v)) = \rho_W(a)f(v)$. We write $\End(V)$ for $\Hom(V, V)$.

Given vector spaces $U$, $V$, and $W$, and linear transformations $f : U \to V$ and $g : V \to W$, we define the following induced maps on the homsets:

  1. $f^* : \Hom(V, W) \to \Hom(U, W)$, where $f^*(\varphi) = \varphi \circ f$.
  2. $g_* : \Hom(U, V) \to \Hom(U, W)$, where $g_*(\varphi) = g \circ \varphi$.

Prove that $f^*$ and $g_*$ are linear transformations.

Let $V$ be a vector space over the field $F$. The dual of $V$ is the homset $\Hom(V, F)$, denoted $V^\dual$.

Given vector spaces $V$ and $W$, and a linear transformation $f : V \to W$, we define its dual to be the linear transformation $f^\dual : W^\dual \to V^\dual, f^\dual(\varphi) = \varphi \circ f$.

Prove that $f^\dual$ is a linear transformation.

Let $V$ and $W$ be finite-dimensional vector spaces. The tensor product of $V$ and $W$, denoted $V \otimes W$, is the homset $\Hom(V^\dual, W)$. For two elements $v \in V$ and $w \in W$, we write $v \otimes w \in V \otimes W$ to mean the element defined by $(v \otimes w)(\varphi) = \varphi(v)w$.

Given vector spaces $U$, $V$, and $W$, and linear transformations $f : U \to V$ and $g : V \to W$, we define the following induced maps on the tensor products:

  1. $f \otimes W : U \otimes W \to V \otimes W$, where $(f \otimes W)(\varphi) = \varphi \circ f^\dual$.
  2. $U \otimes g : U \otimes V \to U \otimes W$, where $(U \otimes g)(\varphi) = g \circ \varphi$.

More generally, given linear transformations $f_i : V_i \to W_i$, $i = 1, 2$, we define $f_1 \otimes f_2 : V_1 \otimes V_2 \to W_1 \otimes W_2$, where $(f_1 \otimes f_2)(\varphi) = f_2 \circ \varphi \circ f_1^\dual$. So $f \otimes W = f \otimes \id_W$ and $U \otimes g = \id_U \otimes g$.

Prove that $f_1 \otimes f_2$ is a linear transformation.

Let $V_0, V_1, \dots, V_n \subseteq V$ be a flag. The $i$th filtration quotient of the flag is the quotient $V_i/V_{i - 1}$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$. The $0$th filtration quotient is conventionally taken to be $V_0 = 0$.

The associated graded of a flag $\FF = (V_0, V_1, \dots, V_n)$ is the direct sum of the filtration quotients $V_1/V_0 \oplus V_2/V_1 \oplus \dots \oplus V_n/V_{n - 1}$, denoted $\gr_\FF V$.

§8Endomorphisms

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space and $B = (v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n)$ be a basis of $V$. For an element $f = \sum_{i, j = 1}^n a_{i, j} v_jv_i^\dual \in \End(V)$, its trace is the element of $F$ given by $\tr(f) = \sum_{i, j = 1}^n a_{i, j} v_i^\dual(v_j)$.

Prove the following properties about the trace:

  1. $\tr : \Hom(V, V) \to F$ is a linear transformation.
  2. For $f : V \to W$ and $g : W \to V$ linear transformations of finite-dimensional $V$ and $W$, $\tr(f \circ g) = \tr(g \circ f)$.
  3. $\tr(f) = \tr([f]_{B \from B})$.

Recall that $\End(V)$ is an algebra as well as a vector space.

  1. Addition of endomorphisms is defined $(f + g)(v) = f(v) + g(v)$.
  2. The additive identity of $\End(V)$ is the zero transformation $0_{\End(V)} : V \to V, 0_{\End(V)}(v) = 0_V$.
  3. Multiplication of endomorphisms is defined $(f * g)(v) = f(g(v))$.
  4. The multiplicative identity of $\End(V)$ is the identity transformation $1_{\End(V)} = \id_V : V \to V$.

Let $V$ be a vector space over $F$. The evaluation homomorphism at $f \in \End(V)$ is the homomorphism \[ \ev_f : F[x] \to \End(V), \quad \ev_f(a_0 + a_1x + \dots + a_nx^n) = a_0 \id_V + a_1 f + \dots + a_n f^n. \] For a polynomial $p(x) \in F[x]$, we write $p(f) \coloneq \ev_f(p(x))$.

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over $F$, and $f : V \to V$ be an endomorphism. Then there exists a nonzero polynomial $p(x) \in F[x]$ such that $p(f) = 0$.

Let $n = \dim V$. The vector space $\End(V)$ has dimension $n^2$, so the sequence $\id_V, f, f^2, f^3, \dots, f^{n^2}$ is linearly dependent. Therefore, there exists a nonzero $p(x) \in F[x]$ with $\deg p(x) \le n^2$ such that $p(f) = 0$.

For an endomorphism $f : V \to V$ of a finite-dimensional vector space $V$, the minimal polynomial of $f$ is the monic polynomial $p(x) \in F[x]$ of least degree such that $p(f) = 0$. We denote the minimal polynomial by $\irr(f, F)$.

Let $p(x) \in F[x]$ be a polynomial such that $p(f) = 0$. Prove that $\irr(f, F) \mid p(x)$.

Let $f : V \to V$ be an endomorphism. An $f$-invariant subspace of $V$ is a subspace $W \subseteq V$ such that $f$ restricts to an endomorphism $f\rvert_W : W \to W$. One may omit the endomorphism and simply say invariant subspace if no confusion may arise.

    2. $P_i^2 = b_i(f) q_i(f) (1 - a_i(f) p_i(f)^{e_i}) = P_i$.
    3. For $i \ne j$, $P_iP_j = b_i(f) q_i(f) b_j(f) q_j(f) = 0$.

§9Bilinear forms

A function $f : U \times V \to W$, where $U$, $V$, and $W$ are vector spaces, is bilinear if

  1. for all $u \in U$, the function $f_u : V \to W, f_u(v) = f(u, v)$ is linear; and
  2. for all $v \in V$, the function $f^v : U \to W, f^v(u) = f(u, v)$ is linear.

A bilinear form on $V$, a vector space over $F$, is a bilinear function $f : V \times V \to F$.

A bilinear form $f : V \times V \to F$ is

  1. symmetric if $f(u, v) = f(v, u)$ for all $u, v \in V$;
  2. alternating if $f(v, v) = 0$ for all $v \in V$;
  3. skew if $f(u, v) = -f(v, u)$ for all $u, v \in V$.
