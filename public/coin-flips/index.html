Coin Flip Court

Enter some coin flips, real or made up. The court runs every test it knows and reports the most suspicious things about your sequence.

How the court works

Each test computes one number from your sequence (say, the longest run of heads) and asks how often $n$ fair, independent flips would produce something at least that extreme. That probability is the $p$-value. The court sorts the tests by $p$-value, so the top row is the most exceptional thing about your flips.

Most are exact. Head counts are binomial, and since each pair of neighbours differs with probability $\tfrac12$, the number of chunks is $1 + \operatorname{Bin}(n-1, \tfrac12)$. Longest runs, alternating stretches and the biggest lead come from a dynamic program over "how long is the current run" (or "how far ahead is heads"), which drains probability into the "it happened" state step by step, so even $p = 2^{-199}$ comes out exactly. Palindromes and pattern counts have no tidy formula, so the court flips a few thousand fair sequences of the same length and counts (marked sim).

Be careful with the verdict. Run eighteen one-sided tests on genuinely random flips and the smallest $p$ will often be below $0.05$. The verdict therefore multiplies the smallest $p$ by the number of tails it looked at (Bonferroni), which is conservative but honest. Humans are usually caught by switching too often: too many chunks, runs that are too short, and too few distinct patterns. Real coins are streakier than people expect.
