How Amog picks the numbers

Arithmetic works like zetamac: operands are uniform within your bounds, subtraction is addition run backwards, $(a + b) - a$, and division is multiplication run backwards, $ab \div a$, so every answer is a whole number. Exponents, logarithms, $P(n, k)$ and $\binom{n}{k}$ have a cap: Amog first picks a base (or $n$) uniformly from those that have at least one valid question under the cap, then a valid exponent (or $k$).

Asking about random numbers would be dull. A random $n$ below $1000$ is prime about one time in six, and $\varphi(p) = p - 1$ is no challenge. So for $\varphi$, $d$ and $\sigma$, Amog usually draws $n$ with probability proportional to $d(n)^2$, where $d(n)$ is the number of divisors, which makes $720$ about $225$ times likelier than a prime. In the other $15\%$ of draws he picks uniformly, so primes still appear now and then (about $3\%$ of the time on $[2, 1000]$).

For $\gcd$ and $\operatorname{lcm}$ he first chooses a common factor $g$ with weight $g\,(m - 2)$, where $m$ is the number of multiples of $g$ in range, then takes two different multiples of $g$, avoiding pairs where one divides the other. On $[2, 200]$ the average $\gcd$ is about $26$, compared with $3.5$ for a random pair. Again, $15\%$ of pairs are uniform, so coprime pairs still show up.

Each correct answer scores one point, and wrong answers cost nothing: the box just waits. Share links contain the whole setup, so a friend who opens yours plays exactly the same game.
